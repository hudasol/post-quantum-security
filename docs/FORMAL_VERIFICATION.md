# Formal verification of the session and update design

Author: Huda Mueen. Generated by `formal/make_doc.py` from `formal/results/proverif_results.csv` (run `python3 -m formal.run_all`, then `python3 -m formal.make_doc`). Do not edit by hand.

Label of every result in this document: **modelled**. A result is a proof or a counterexample found by ProVerif (version 2.05, pinned in `formal/install.sh`) for an abstract model of the design. It is not a test of any implementation, and nothing cryptographic is implemented in this repository.

## 1. What was checked

The models follow `docs/REFERENCE_ARCHITECTURE.md` (sections 1 and 5), the architecture definitions in `mission/model.py` (architectures B and D) and the rekey assumption of `linksim/protocols.py` (a fresh key exchange inside the existing session, without signatures). Where the architecture text is silent, the model states the choice it makes (section 3). Four parts were modelled:

1. **Command channel (architecture B).** The ground station sends a classical Diffie Hellman share and a fresh KEM public key; the vehicle answers with its own share and a KEM ciphertext; the ground station signs the transcript and confirms the key; both derive the session key from the Diffie Hellman secret, the KEM secret and a hash of the transcript. Commands then carry a MAC tag and a sequence number under that key. A rekey runs a fresh exchange, with a fresh KEM key, protected by the current key. After a link outage the session is torn down and a new handshake starts.
2. **Fallback with provisioned keys (architecture D, session half).** A key loaded over a wire is used as the signing key in every session.
3. **Firmware update (architecture D, boot half).** An image and its version are signed with an abstract stateful hash based signature (LMS) and verified by the bootloader, with and without a monotonic version counter in protected storage.
4. **KEM authenticated session (design K, round 6).** The same hybrid exchange, but each side is authenticated by a static KEM key pinned at the other side and by key confirmation, without signatures (section 10).

In total 39 model files and 240 queries were run: 102 proved, 126 counterexamples and 12 that the tool could not prove. The counterexamples include the control rows in which both components are broken on purpose (section 7), the stated designs before each change (section 6), and the controls and reachability checks of the replay rules (section 9), the reachability checks and the settings with a broken KEM or known static keys of design K (section 10), and the reachability checks of the session models and the settings with a broken signature (sections 7.1 and 7.2). The session and fallback models allow an unbounded number of sessions; the firmware models allow an unbounded number of devices, each booting three times, with three signed releases. The replay rule models of section 9 state their own bounds on commands per key, and section 11 lists the bounds of every model.

## 2. Assumptions and what is not modelled

- **Perfect primitives.** Signatures, the Diffie Hellman group, the KEM, the hash, the key derivation and the MAC are abstract functions that cannot be broken unless a model gives a secret to the attacker on purpose. The 6 byte truncation of the MAVLink 2 tag is not represented; its forgery probability is a separate, quantitative question.
- **Dolev Yao attacker.** The attacker reads, deletes, delays, replays and injects any message on the link, and runs any number of sessions in parallel with the honest parties.
- **Broken components.** A broken classical component is modelled by giving every Diffie Hellman exponent to the attacker; a broken KEM by giving every KEM secret key to the attacker.
- **Forward secrecy.** Long term keys are given to the attacker in a second phase, after all sessions of the first phase have ended; the query asks whether keys of the first phase become known.
- **Not modelled:** timing, clocks and the one minute timestamp window; side channels; implementation bugs; denial of service and jamming (the attacker can always stop the link, so availability is studied in `docs/MISSION_STUDY.md`, not here); the 30 s grace period in which the old key stays valid during a rekey; compromise of a third party identity (one ground station and one vehicle identity); key provisioning and storage; the state management of the LMS signer; physical access to the protected counter.

## 3. Modelling choices

- **Replay rule.** MAVLink 2 accepts a signed message if its timestamp is greater than the last one on the same stream (`data/raw/S3_robotics.md`, line 25). ProVerif abstracts state, and with a greater than rule and gaps it could not prove injective agreement (row `session_timestamp_rule` in section 5; the non injective form holds). The other models therefore use a stricter rule: the vehicle accepts only the next sequence number. This is a limit of the abstraction, not a finding about the design; the result for the published rule is recorded as cannot be proved, not as proved. Rules that accept commands out of order (a replay cache and a bounded window) are modelled separately in section 9.
- **Session length.** Each session model of sections 6 and 7 carries one command, a rekey and one command under the new key, then ends. The replay rule models of section 9 carry several commands per key (bounds in section 9). A new handshake is a new copy of the process, so outage, teardown and a new session are covered by the unbounded number of copies.
- **Ground station key pair for the KEM.** In architecture B the ground station sends the KEM public key and the vehicle encapsulates (`mission/model.py`, flights of architecture B).
- **Persisted replay state with provisioned keys.** The cited guidance recommends persisting the timestamp but notes that stored timestamps often stay at the provisioning value because vehicles are powered off by pulling the battery (`data/raw/S3_robotics.md`, line 37). The fallback model therefore restarts the replay state of the vehicle at each power cycle.

## 6. Design findings and changes

### F1. The vehicle is not authenticated to the ground station

Stated design: A2 counterexample, S2 counterexample, S4 counterexample; A1 proved, S1 proved, C1 proved.

What the tool showed: only the ground station signs, so anyone can answer its first message in place of the vehicle. The ground station then completes a session and holds a key that it shares with that other party, not with the vehicle. The vehicle side is unaffected: the vehicle still accepts only sessions signed by the ground station and only commands the ground station sent. The risk is on the ground side: telemetry and acknowledgements in such a session do not come from the vehicle.

Change: the vehicle signs the transcript with its own long term key (ML-DSA-44, pinned at the ground station), both signatures cover both identities, and both sides send a key confirmation tag. After the change: A1 proved, A2 proved, S1 proved, S2 proved, S3 proved, S4 proved, C1 proved, C2 proved. Cost: one more signature on the down link and one more verification on the ground station; the vehicle signs once per handshake.

### F2. Provisioned keys allow replay after a power cycle

Stated fallback: C1 counterexample.

What the tool showed: the same provisioned key signs every session, so a command recorded in one session carries a valid tag in the next one. When the replay state of the vehicle restarts after a power cycle, the recorded command is accepted a second time.

Change: at every session start the vehicle sends a fresh nonce, the ground station answers with its own nonce under a tag of the provisioned key, and both derive the session key from the provisioned key and both nonces; sequence numbers are then bound to the session. After the change: C1 proved, C2 proved, S1 proved.

Remaining limit (not removable without a key exchange): FS counterexample for the provisioned design. Whoever later obtains the provisioned key can recompute the keys of earlier sessions from the recorded nonces. The architecture already states that a captured vehicle exposes these keys until they expire; provisioned keys should therefore be short lived and the fallback used only where B cannot finish.

### F3. Firmware downgrade without a version counter

Stated design (signature over image and version, no counter): D1 counterexample. With a counter but an unsigned version: D1 counterexample. With a counter and a signed version: D1 proved.

What the tool showed: a valid signature only says that the vendor once released the image. Without a counter, an older signed image is accepted after a newer one. With a counter but a version outside the signed data, an older image can be offered with a higher claimed version.

Change: the signed data covers the image and its version, and the bootloader keeps a monotonic counter in protected storage, boots only a version at least equal to the counter and then raises the counter.

The one line goal statement that ProVerif printed for each counterexample is kept in `formal/results/logs/<model>.txt`.

### 7.2 Signature component broken

The hybrid exchange does not help here: both secrets of the key are shared with the attacker, who took part in the exchange. What remains is the matched session: SM proved, so a session in which the attacker did not take the place of either side keeps its key secret (an attacker who breaks ML-DSA-44 later, or who only records, learns nothing from it).

- **Consequence for the design.** The hybrid exchange protects the confidentiality of honest sessions against a break of either exchange component, but not against an active attacker who can forge ML-DSA-44 signatures. A design that must survive a break of ML-DSA-44 needs a second, independent authentication: for example a classical signature beside ML-DSA-44 (a hybrid signature), or the KEM authentication of design K in addition to the signatures (section 10 shows that design K alone in turn rests on ML-KEM).

## 8. Text the reference architecture should adopt

1. Session start: "The vehicle also signs the transcript with its own ML-DSA-44 key, pinned at the ground station; both signatures cover both identities and the full transcript, and each side sends a key confirmation tag before it accepts the session." (F1)
2. Key derivation: "The session key is derived from the X25519 secret, the ML-KEM-768 secret and a hash of the transcript; a rekey uses a fresh ML-KEM key pair and fresh X25519 shares, protected by the current key." (already implied; proved in section 7)
3. Fallback: "Provisioned keys are never used directly as signing keys. At every session start both sides exchange fresh nonces and derive the session signing key from the provisioned key and both nonces, so that sequence numbers and timestamps are bound to the session." (F2)
4. Boot: "The LMS signature covers the image and its version. The bootloader keeps a monotonic version counter in protected storage, boots only an image whose signed version is at least the counter, and then raises the counter." (F3)
5. Limits: "The formal check covers an abstract model only (docs/FORMAL_VERIFICATION.md); it does not cover timing, side channels, implementation or denial of service."

## 9. Replay rules that accept commands out of order

The open design question of `docs/LIMITATIONS_AND_PLAN_R6.md` (item L4) is whether the vehicle may accept commands out of order. Sections 3 and 6 prove C1 for the strict next sequence rule only, and C1 could not be proved for the published timestamp rule. The models of this section replace only the rule by which the vehicle accepts a command. The tool behaviour cited below is taken from the ProVerif 2.05 manual (`data/raw/proverif_manual_notes.md`).

### 9.1 Encodings

- **Seen counter table as a restriction** (models `session_replay_cache`, `session_replay_cache_fresh_counters`, `session_replay_window`). Every branch of the vehicle that accepts a command draws a fresh stamp and records the event Mark(session, counter, stamp). A restriction keeps only the traces in which no two branches record the same session and counter with different stamps; ProVerif proves the queries on exactly those traces (manual, section 6.2). A branch that would accept a pair already accepted therefore cannot proceed, which is the behaviour of a vehicle that looks the pair up in a table of accepted pairs and inserts it in one indivisible step, with a table that never forgets an entry and is keyed by the session identifier. The restriction is an assumption about the vehicle, not something the tool proves. What the tool proves under it is that every accepted command was sent by the ground station in that session (the tag and the session binding), and, with counters that the sender never repeats under one key, that each sent command is accepted at most once.
- **ProVerif tables** (models `session_replay_cache_table_concurrent` and `session_replay_cache_table_sequential`). The vehicle runs `get seen(=session, =counter) in (drop) else insert seen(session, counter); accept`. Records cannot be deleted from a table (manual, section 4.1.5), so a table can express a cache that never forgets but not a window that evicts old counters. In the concurrent model the vehicle handles command messages in parallel branches, so the lookup and the insert are two steps that other branches can interleave. In the sequential model the vehicle reads command messages one after the other.
- **Bounded window** (models `session_replay_window` and `session_replay_window_no_restriction`, W = 2). The vehicle reads command messages one after the other and keeps in process variables the highest accepted counter and the counters accepted so far. It accepts counter t if the tag is valid, t is greater than the highest accepted counter minus W, and t was not accepted before. Inside the bound on messages the state is exact, and every counter at or below the highest minus W is rejected by the window test, as in a window that keeps a bitmap of the last W counters. In `session_replay_window` the seen counter restriction is added to help the tool; it removes no trace of this process, because the process itself never accepts a counter twice under one key (a hand argument from the process text, not checked by the tool). `session_replay_window_no_restriction` is the same rule without that help.
- **Control** (model `session_replay_cache_broken_no_check`). The vehicle accepts any command with a valid tag and does not consult a table.
- **Full handshake or command layer.** The models with parallel acceptance branches keep the whole session with change F1 (hybrid exchange, both transcript signatures, key confirmation, rekey) and ask the secrecy queries of section 4 as a sanity check. The models that read messages one after the other exhausted the memory of the run host inside the full handshake, so they are written on the command layer only, in the style of `session_timestamp_rule`: each session is a fresh abstract key and a fresh session identifier. Their secrecy query S0 holds by construction and only checks that the command layer does not send the key.
- **Commands per key.** The ground station sends several commands per key, with counters starting at 1 under each key; in `session_replay_cache_fresh_counters` it sends any number of commands per key. In the full handshake models, commands under the first key are accepted in parallel with the rekey, so an old key command may still be accepted after the rekey; this is a longer grace period than the 30 s of the architecture and therefore gives the attacker at least as much room.

### 9.4 Reading

- **Replay cache, indivisible lookup and insert.** C1 proved and C2 proved with 3 commands per key, and C1 proved and C2 proved with any number of commands per key, in the full handshake. The session keys stay secret in the same model (S1 proved, S2 proved, S3 proved, S4 proved), and the handshake agreement queries of `session_b_fixed` hold as well (A1 proved, A2 proved; with any number of commands per key A1 proved, A2 proved), so adding the rule does not disturb the handshake. The vehicle does accept commands in these models (R0 counterexample and counterexample, a counterexample meaning that acceptance is reachable), so C1 and C2 are not proved vacuously. In the model, a vehicle may therefore accept commands out of order and with gaps, provided it remembers every accepted (session, counter) pair for the life of the key and checks and records a pair in one step.
- **Control without the check.** C1 counterexample (weaker form: non injective form proved): the attacker delivers a recorded command twice and the vehicle accepts it twice. The proof of the cache model therefore rests on the table, not on the rest of the model.
- **Tables, lookup and insert in two steps.** C1 counterexample: two branches of the vehicle both find no entry for the same command, both insert it, and both accept it (the goal statement of the trace is in `formal/results/logs/session_replay_cache_table_concurrent.txt`). A vehicle that checks and records counters on two receive paths at once (two radios, or a check and a store in different tasks) needs the lookup and the insert to be one indivisible step.
- **Tables, one message after the other.** C1 cannot be proved (weaker form: non injective form proved). From the manual: the tool ignores how often an action repeats, because processes are translated into Horn clauses, and this can yield false attacks (manual, section 6.2). The author's reading, which the manual does not state: the abstraction therefore does not keep the fact that a table entry is absent when the else branch of a lookup runs, so a second acceptance of the same command cannot be ruled out (the derivation in the log of this model reaches the same acceptance event at two occurrences). As for the published timestamp rule in section 3, this is a limit of the tool, not a finding about the design, and it is recorded as such.
- **Bounded window, W = 2.** C1 proved and C2 proved with the restriction, inside the bound of 4 command messages per key. Without the restriction C1 cannot be proved (weaker form: non injective form proved) and C2 proved, for the same reason as the sequential table. R1 counterexample: the vehicle does accept a command older than the highest accepted one, so the model is not the strict rule in disguise.
- **Reachability in every replay rule model.** R0 results: `session_replay_cache` counterexample, `session_replay_cache_fresh_counters` counterexample, `session_replay_window` counterexample, `session_replay_window_no_restriction` counterexample, `session_replay_cache_table_sequential` counterexample, `session_replay_cache_table_concurrent` counterexample, `session_replay_cache_broken_no_check` counterexample. A counterexample to R0 is the expected result: it is a trace in which the vehicle accepts a command, so every proof of C1 and C2 in this section is about a model in which commands are accepted.
- **Handshake agreement in the other full handshake models.** In the control `session_replay_cache_broken_no_check` A1 proved and A2 proved; in `session_replay_cache_table_concurrent` A1 proved and A2 proved. The replay rule acts after the handshake, so the handshake results do not depend on it.

### 9.5 What these proofs do not say about a real replay window

- A real window forgets counters that fall out of it. The cache models never forget, and the window model keeps every counter it accepted within its bound on messages. The proof covers a real window only through the window test, which rejects every counter at or below the highest accepted minus W; a cache that evicts entries without such a floor is in the position of the control model.
- Counters are unbounded natural numbers. Finite counter width, wrap around and the 48 bit MAVLink timestamp field are not represented.
- The vehicle state is never lost inside a key. A reset of the table while the same key stays in use (a reboot that keeps the key) is not modelled; in architecture B and in the fallback with change F2 every reboot starts a new handshake and therefore a new session identifier.
- The window and sequential table models are bounded in the number of command messages the vehicle reads per key, attacker messages included, and cover the command layer only; an attack that needs more deliveries under one key is outside them.
- The restriction is an assumption that the implementation checks and records a pair in one step and keeps the table for the life of the key; the tool does not prove it, and the concurrent table model shows what happens without it.
- Availability is not covered: a window rejects late commands that a cache would accept, and the cost of that in lost commands is a question for the mission and flight stack studies, not for this model.

## 10. KEM authenticated hybrid session (design K)

Round 6 adds to the RTL harness a session in which signatures are replaced by KEM authentication with pinned static keys. This section checks the design of that session in the same way as design B with change F1 (sections 6 and 7). Only the design is modelled; the harness code is not checked by these models.

### 10.1 Messages

The ground station and the vehicle each hold a static ML-KEM-768 key pair; each static public key is pinned at the other side, as the ML-DSA-44 keys are in design B.

1. **F1, ground station to vehicle:** an ephemeral X25519 share, an ephemeral ML-KEM-768 public key, and a ciphertext encapsulated to the static key of the vehicle.
2. **F2, vehicle to ground station:** the X25519 share of the vehicle, a ciphertext encapsulated to the ephemeral key of the ground station, a ciphertext encapsulated to the static key of the ground station, and the key confirmation tag of the vehicle.
3. **F3, ground station to vehicle:** the key confirmation tag of the ground station.

The session key is derived from the Diffie Hellman secret, the three KEM secrets and a hash of the transcript, which includes both identities and the flights F1 and F2 without the vehicle tag, as in the KEM authenticated design of the RTL harness (`rtlsim/proto/sw/proto_bench.c`, design kem_auth, with the KEM secrets in the same order: vehicle static, ground ephemeral, ground static). Only the holder of the vehicle static key can compute the first KEM secret, so a valid vehicle tag authenticates the vehicle; only the holder of the ground static key can compute the third, so a valid ground tag authenticates the ground station. Commands then use the per message tag under the strict next sequence rule with one command, a rekey and one command under the new key, as in the session models (`session_kemauth` and its variants), or the replay cache rule of section 9 with 3 commands per key (`session_kemauth_replay_cache`). The rekey is the one of design B: fresh X25519 shares and a fresh ephemeral KEM key, protected by the current key.

Modelling choices specific to design K: decapsulation of a ciphertext that was not made for the key returns a value that only the holder of the secret key can compute (implicit rejection), so the vehicle answers any F1; the KEM secret of the ground station for the ciphertext of F1 is the one it encapsulated. The broken KEM setting gives the attacker every KEM secret key, static and ephemeral, from the start, which is what a break of ML-KEM means for this design; the forward secrecy settings give the two static KEM secret keys to the attacker after all sessions have ended.

Two choices help the tool finish; both are stated in the model files. First, the names that the vehicle draws after it reads F1 are written `new y[]`, the construct of ProVerif that sets which arguments the internal representation of a name has (CHANGES file of the ProVerif source, version 1.90). Each run still draws a fresh value. With the default representation the tool did not finish even S1 on the run host, because the attacker can pass a ciphertext made in one vehicle session into F1 of another session and the representation grows with each pass. In the two settings with a broken KEM the names that the ground station draws after it reads F2 are written the same way: without it those settings did not finish S1, and with it the setting without a broken component did not finish within the time limit, so it is used only there. The author's reading, not checked by the tool, is that this changes the precision of the abstraction and not the set of attacks it covers. Second, A2 is asked in a file of its own: in one file with the other queries it kept the whole file from finishing within the time limit of `formal/run_all.py` (1800 s). A2 is asked twice: in `session_kemauth_a2`, with the same model as the other queries, and in `session_kemauth_a2_typed_static`, in which static and ephemeral KEM keys have distinct types, so the attacker cannot offer a pinned static public key in place of the ground ephemeral key (a restriction of the attacker that the real design does not have; it removes the chains of passes described above).

The author's reading, not confirmed by the tool, is that an attacker who holds both static keys can play the ground station to the vehicle, as in the trace of S1, so A1 is not expected to hold in these settings. Together with `session_kemauth_kem_broken`, this shows that design K, like design B, rests its authentication on one post quantum primitive, here ML-KEM, and keeps only the confidentiality of matched sessions when it fails.
- **General A2 asked again.** `session_kemauth_a2_retry` asks A2 of the same process as `session_kemauth_a2` (which gave cannot be proved (timeout 1800 s)), with the tool setting `set selFun = Term.` (the selection function of the resolution, a documented setting of ProVerif that changes how the tool searches and not the model), in one run with a time limit of 10800 s and a memory cap of 6144 MB; it did not finish either (cannot be proved (timeout 10800 s)), so A2 of the general model stays open, as recorded in section 10.3; no attack was found. The choice of setting came from short trial runs of 120 s to 300 s each, not recorded as results: with the default selection function the tool inserted about 2,000 clauses in 300 s, at about the same rate with the non injective form of A2, with `set maxDepth = 8.` or `12`, or with the names of the ground station written without arguments; with `selFun = Term` it inserted about 18,000 in 300 s, and with `redundancyElim = no` about 9,000 in 120 s, in both cases with the queue of clauses still growing; `selFun = Nounifset` stopped at a termination warning that waits for input. The setting with the highest rate was chosen; a higher rate does not show that the tool will finish.

### 10.5 Not covered for design K

- Compromise of one static key only. Whoever holds the static KEM key of the vehicle can compute the first KEM secret, and whoever holds that of the ground station the third; whether a stolen static key of one side lets the attacker pose as the other side to it (key compromise impersonation) was not asked.
- Everything listed in section 2 as not modelled, including the truncation of the tag, the grace period of a rekey, clocks and the width of the counter.
- Provisioning, storage and rotation of the static KEM keys.

## 11. Bounds of every model

Every model allows an unbounded number of sessions (or devices) in parallel with the attacker. The bounds below are the work inside one session or one device.

| Models | Handshake | Keys per session | Commands per key | Command messages read per key at the vehicle | Replay rule |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| session_b_* (including the settings with a broken signature of section 7.2) | design B, as stated or with change F1 | 2 (one rekey) | 1 | 1 | next sequence number |
| session_kemauth (and its broken, forward secrecy, known static key and A2 variants) | design K | 2 (one rekey) | 1 | 1 | next sequence number |
| session_kemauth_replay_cache | design K | 2 (one rekey) | 3 | unbounded | replay cache (restriction) |
| fallback_d_* | provisioned key, with or without change F2 | 1 | 2 | 2 | next sequence number |
| session_timestamp_rule | none (abstract key) | 1 | 2 (counters 1 and 3) | 2 | published timestamp rule |
| session_replay_* | see section 9.2 | 1 or 2 | 3, 4 or unbounded | 4 or unbounded | see section 9.2 |
| firmware_* | none | not applicable | three signed releases | three boots per device | version counter or none |
