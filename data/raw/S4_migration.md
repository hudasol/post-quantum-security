## 2. Topic 1: Migrating deployed, long-lived embedded/IoT/OT fleets

- [source] NCSC (A6) sets 2028 for discovery and an initial plan, 2031 for the highest-priority migrations, and 2035 for completing migration. It says a small set of rarely used technologies may find 2035 harder. Published 2025-03-20; treat as "as of 2026-10-11" and not revised as far as I saw.
- [source] NCSC (A6) says the 2028 plan should flag any requirement to migrate long-lived hardware roots of trust, and to record dependencies on suppliers and physical infrastructure.
- [source] NCSC (A6) lists strategy options per system: in-place migration, re-platform, retire, run until end of life, or tolerate the risk. It also acknowledges legacy systems (long-lived physical infrastructure, outdated protocols) that cannot be transitioned to PQC and says strategy must account for them.
- [source] NCSC (A6) says industrial IoT devices need special attention because they may not be upgradeable, may be embedded in larger products, may not be designed to be replaced, and may use proprietary or not-yet-PQC-compatible protocols. It also says certificate-based PKI and IoT/ICS protocols will become PQC-ready more slowly than confidentiality services.
- [source] NCSC (A6) says to expect traditional and PQC cryptography to co-exist for a time, to seek cryptographic agility, and to define criteria for ending support for traditional algorithms; full quantum security needs no sole dependence on traditional public-key cryptography.
- [source] NCSC (A6), PKI: migration may need a new PQC root of trust, either run in parallel with the old PKI or a new root that cross-signs the old one; some devices may need physical interaction. Authentication is not quantum-secure until the PKI migration is complete and traditional certificates have expired or been revoked.
- [source] EU roadmap (A7): Member States to start national strategies by end 2026; high-risk use cases transitioned by end 2030; remaining (low-risk) use cases by end 2035 "as much as feasible". Recommends standardised, tested hybrid solutions where feasible. In flux: page updated 2026-09-03 and survey feedback published 2026-09-02.
- [source] EU roadmap (A7): products entering the market with an expected lifetime beyond 2030 should be upgradable to PQC, and the software/firmware upgrade mechanism should use post-quantum signature schemes for integrity and authenticity. It links this to Cyber Resilience Act crypto-agility requirements applying systematically from December 2027.
- [source] EU roadmap (A7) risk logic: a use case with transition effort over 8 years and high impact (example given: securing software updates) is rated high risk. It says that implies quantum-safe signatures on software and firmware updates "as soon as possible".
- [source] CISA/NSA/NIST fact sheet (A3, 2023-08-17): build a quantum-readiness roadmap, create a cryptographic inventory (including firmware and software update mechanisms and embedded cryptography, which discovery tools may miss, so ask vendors), discuss vendor roadmaps, and write contracts so new products ship with PQC and older products are upgraded. It names signature creation/validation including software and firmware updates as quantum-vulnerable assets.
- [source] NIST CSWP 39 (A2): defines crypto agility as the capability to replace and adapt algorithms in protocols, applications, software, hardware, firmware and infrastructure while preserving operations. It describes a "crypto gateway" (bump-in-the-wire) for legacy systems whose crypto cannot be modified, so new algorithms are deployed without changing each legacy component (section 4.6).
- [source] NIST SP 1800-38A (A1, preliminary draft, in flux): most systems are not designed for rapid algorithm change; new algorithms will likely not be drop-in because key and signature sizes, execution steps and key-establishment complexity differ; interim hybrid implementations may be needed. Only discovery-tooling and interoperability workstreams are described; no embedded-fleet guidance in the version I read.
- [source] Uptane 2.0.0 (A18) (automotive OTA): Root role distributes and revokes keys of the other roles; compromise-resilience via thresholds of keys per role, version and release counters against rollback. The text I searched shows key algorithm as an attribute (e.g. RSA or ECDSA) and no PQC discussion. [inference] Uptane's key-role and threshold structure is a model for revocable, delegated signing keys under one trust anchor, but I found no Uptane statement on PQC migration.
- [source] UAV review (A21, 2024): lists PQC challenges for UAVs: standards still evolving, compatibility needing hardware and software updates for interoperability with legacy systems, multi-vendor consensus, and size/weight limits that make adding processing or memory hard. Hybrid schemes and hardware acceleration are proposed directions. It is a review, not a measurement paper.
- [source] Banegas et al. (A20, 2021 preprint): in a RIOT/SUIT case study on a Cortex-M4 board (nRF52840), crypto was under 15% of a roughly 50 kB firmware flash footprint with Ed25519; PQ signatures are described as far from drop-in replacements for microcontrollers. Pre-standard (round 3 Dilithium/Falcon, LMS RFC 8554), so any size figures carry a pre-standard warning; S2 owns the benchmark numbers.

Not retrieved for this topic: ISO/SAE 21434 and AUTOSAR text; ENISA publications as such (the EU roadmap above is the NIS Cooperation Group document); Uptane 2.1.0; a UAV-fleet-specific migration paper (the MDPI Drones 2025 search hit returned 403).

## 3. Topic 2: Firmware-signing migration vs session-key migration

### 3.1 Why they differ

- [source] NIST IR 8547 ipd (A4, section 3.1.1): for code signing, devices that install code must verify its signatures; in some cases the verification code cannot be updated after manufacture, and then devices should be designed to require quantum-resistant signatures if they may still be in use once a cryptographically relevant quantum computer (CRQC) exists. In flux (draft).
- [source] NIST CSWP 39 (A2, section 4.5): for environments where hardware cryptography cannot be updated, use the most conservative variants. Its example is secure boot: the public key and verification program live in boot code that cannot be updated, so the signature scheme must stay secure for the device lifetime.
- [source] NIST SP 800-208 (A5, section 1.1): stateful hash-based signatures (LMS/XMSS) are not for general use because of state management, but suit applications that need a signature scheme soon, have long lifetimes, and cannot practically change scheme once deployed. It names authentication of firmware updates for constrained devices that may be in use for decades as a fit.
- [source] RFC 9019 (A8): recommends unchangeable bootloader code in ROM use post-quantum signatures such as hash-based ones (RFC 8778), and says a bootloader author must weigh service lifetime against the quantum time horizon. The RFC also states a worst-case horizon of about 2030 "at the time of writing" (2021); that figure is historic and should not be repeated as a current estimate (see topic 5).
- [source] RFC 9019 (A8): the manifest specification must support algorithm extensibility; the code that verifies signatures on a manifest is typically in application firmware rather than the bootloader, and crypto may run in a separate secure element or HSM.
- [source] NIST IR 8547 ipd (A4, section 3.1.2): for authentication, unlike encryption, there is no harvest-now-decrypt-later threat; an authentication system stays secure as long as the algorithms and keys are secure when the authentication is performed, and quantum-vulnerable authentication must be disabled once capable quantum computers exist.
- [source] Banegas et al. (A20, section 3.3.1): a quantum breakthrough at time T does not affect elliptic-curve signatures made before T, but allows forging signatures after T. If the update distributor's key has a lifetime as long as the devices, it must resist quantum attack.
- [inference] Session keys are ephemeral and renegotiated on every connection, so changing the key-exchange algorithm is a change to software on both ends, deployable per connection, with fallback; but past recorded traffic stays exposed unless PQC is used from the start of that traffic. Firmware signing is a long-lived verification key anchored in the device; if that anchor sits in immutable ROM or OTP, the exposure is future forgery against every unit still trusting it. This asymmetry (confidentiality risk is retroactive, authenticity risk is prospective) follows from the IR 8547 and Banegas statements above but is my synthesis.

### 3.2 Root of trust, revocation, hybrid images, rollback

- [source] MCUboot (A17): the `MCUBOOT_HW_KEY` option keeps only a hash of the public key in the device (provisioned by the target); the image carries the full public key, which is hashed and compared at boot. The documented signature TLV types I saw list RSA and Ed25519 types in the lines I read and no post-quantum type. [inference] This is a snapshot of `design.md` on the main branch; PQ support may exist elsewhere and I did not find it in the file.
- [source] MCUboot (A17): hardware-based rollback protection uses a security counter in the signed image metadata, compared with a counter held in non-volatile trusted storage; downgrade prevention is also available in software via version comparison.
- [source] wolfBoot (A16, vendor docs): supports ML-DSA (three parameter sets) and LMS/HSS and XMSS/XMSS^MT; a "hybrid mode" verifies a classical and a PQ signature sequentially using a secondary-signature setting and a keystore that holds both key types. The README changelog also mentions anti-rollback by version number, support for multiple public keys in a keystore, and OTP keystore provisioning tools. The doc states ML-DSA public keys are larger than LMS/XMSS ones and that stateful keys need more care in key generation and signing.
- [source] wolfBoot (A16) example configuration for ML-DSA-44 sets a 2420-byte signature and a 4840-byte image header; the LMS example (2 levels, height 5, Winternitz 8) sets a 2644-byte signature and a 5288-byte header. These are example config values from the project docs, not measurements, and the setups are not directly comparable to each other.
- [source] wolfBoot (A16) says hash-based signatures are recommended by NIST SP 800-208 and "the NSA's CNSA 2.0 suite". [inference] This is a vendor restatement; I could not retrieve the NSA documents, so no CNSA 2.0 timeline is asserted here.
- [source] IETF SUIT: RFC 9124 (A9) requires that a manifest format be able to carry multiple signatures so several parties' authorisations can be required (requirement REQ.USE.MFST.MULTI_AUTH). [inference] That is the format-level hook a dual (classical plus PQ) signature scheme would use; the RFC does not describe it as a PQ transition mechanism.
- [source] RFC 9124 (A9): signing keys should be replaced from time to time, and because replacing a trust anchor is difficult and risky, update-signing keys should be delegates of the trust anchor. Expiry by time needs a secure clock. A firmware update is by definition remote code execution; rollback checks help but cannot defend against a compromised trusted signer.
- [source] SUIT MTI draft -23 (A10, in flux): recommends receivers implement the profile that uses HSS/LMS with SHA-256 for authentication, as an alternative to ESP256-based profiles; recommends deeper trees to support the long lifetimes of IoT devices; notes library or hardware availability for PQ algorithms as a selection factor.
- [source] RFC 8778 (A15, status header only) defines HSS/LMS in COSE and RFC 9964 (A14, status header only) defines ML-DSA in COSE/JOSE (Proposed Standard, 2026-05). [inference] Together these mean standard COSE code points exist for both LMS and ML-DSA signatures, so SUIT manifests can in principle carry them; I did not read the RFC bodies.
- [source] NCSC (A6): some vendors already ship secure boot based on LMS/XMSS; hardware roots of trust and secure boot using the new FIPS algorithms were expected later in 2025, with efficiency improving in 2026-27 as hardware acceleration arrives. [inference] This is an expectation stated in March 2025; I did not verify what shipped.
- [source] NIST CSWP 39 (A2, section 3.2.4): a hybrid signature needs both a classical and a PQC public key certified, either in one certificate or via separate roots of trust. Separate roots mean you can later stop issuing under the classical root while the PQC anchor continues. Hybrids add protocol complexity; moving to a hybrid root and then to PQC-only could mean two transitions.
- [source] NIST IR 8547 ipd (A4, section 3.2): hybrids are expected to be temporary and lead to a second transition; NIST leaves it to each application to decide if the cost and complexity are acceptable and will accommodate hybrid and dual signatures in FIPS 140 validation when combined with an approved scheme.

### 3.3 Stateful hash-based signatures (SP 800-208), conceptual

- [source] SP 800-208 (A5, section 1.2): the private key is a large set of one-time keys; if any one-time key signs two different messages, an attacker holding both signatures can feasibly forge signatures on arbitrary messages. Signing should therefore happen in a highly controlled environment.
- [source] SP 800-208 (A5, abstract/section 8): key and signature generation must be done in hardware cryptographic modules that do not allow secret key material to be exported, even encrypted. This is why ordinary key backup (copying the private key) is not allowed.
- [source] SP 800-208 (A5, section 7): to survive loss of a signing module without copying keys, use multiple keys on different modules and have clients accept any of them, with new public keys distributed over time (it cites the Hash Of Root Key certificate extension), or use a two-level multi-tree scheme (HSS or XMSS^MT) where sub-trees live on different modules and the top-level module is a single point of failure.
- [inference] Practical consequence for a drone fleet: the build/release signing process, not the drone, holds the state. The risk is operational (VM snapshots, restores, parallel CI signers, HSM failover) rather than on-device. The device only verifies, so a stateful scheme costs the vendor process rather than the airframe.

### 3.4 Session-key migration (TLS/DTLS/IKE/Noise/MAVLink)

- [source] RFC 10024 (A11, Proposed Standard, 2026-08): defines hybrid key agreement groups for TLS 1.3 combining ML-KEM with ECDHE: X25519MLKEM768, SecP256r1MLKEM768, SecP384r1MLKEM1024. Client key-share sizes are 1216, 1249 and 1665 bytes respectively; server shares 1120, 1153 and 1665 bytes. Its IANA entries mark all three as usable with DTLS.
- [source] draft-ietf-tls-mlkem-11 (A12, active draft, in flux): ML-KEM-only key agreement for TLS 1.3, last revised 2026-09-16. Not an RFC as of retrieval.
- [source] RFC 9370 (A13, 2023-05): extends IKEv2 so several key exchanges are combined while setting up or rekeying an SA, using IKE_INTERMEDIATE and IKE_FOLLOWUP_KE. [inference] This is the standards-track way to add a PQ KEM alongside classical ECDH in IPsec tunnels used between ground and fleet gateways.
- [source] NCSC (A6): mainstream browsers already negotiate ML-KEM hybrids, but the mechanism was not yet standardised industry-wide at publication; IETF TLS standards were expected around 2027 (RFC 10024 appeared in 2026-08, earlier than that expectation).
- [source] MAVLink signing (A26): the 48-bit signature is the first 48 bits of a SHA-256 over the packet, timestamp and a 32-byte shared secret key. The key is distributed with the `SETUP_SIGNING` message, which should be sent only over a secure link such as USB or wired Ethernet. The protocol page describes authentication only; I saw no mention of encryption on that page. [inference] Symmetric-key authentication of this kind is not what Shor's algorithm breaks directly, but the confidentiality and key-provisioning paths around it (how the shared secret reaches the vehicle) matter for HNDL; the page's rule to use a secure physical link is a mitigation for provisioning, not a PQC feature. No MAVLink key-exchange standard was found in this stream (S3 owns MAVLink protocol detail).
- Noise protocol PQ variants: not retrieved.

### 3.5 Key and signature size impact (source figures)

- [source] ML-DSA sizes from wolfBoot docs (A16): public key / signature of ML-DSA-44 1312 / 2420 bytes, ML-DSA-65 1952 / 3309, ML-DSA-87 2592 / 4627. LMS in Banegas et al. (A20): 60-byte public key, 4756-byte signature for the parameters used there. Ed25519 in the same paper: 32-byte keys, 64-byte signature. Hybrid TLS client shares above show the handshake size effect (about 1.2 to 1.7 kB).
- [source] CSWP 39 (A2, section 6.1): large sizes challenge existing protocols; protocol designers should separate intrinsic limits from short-sighted design choices. Buffers and storage should not assume algorithm-specific sizes.

## 4. Topic 3: Firmware update vs hardware replacement

Everything in this section is tagged by whether a source says it or I infer it.

- [source] CSWP 39 (A2, section 4.5): functions implemented in an integrated circuit cannot be changed, so a chip must be replaced if the algorithms must change; non-removable secure chips are purposefully difficult to replace; some devices permit upgrading functionality in place; FPGAs are the exception because they are reconfigurable. Once a chip leaves the factory, adding algorithms is not easy; software libraries are easier to make agile than hardware.
- [source] CSWP 39 (A2, section 6.1): hardware capacity limits how many algorithms one platform can hold; accelerator reuse across algorithms needs further research for the traditional-to-PQC transition.
- [source] NIST IR 8547 ipd (A4, section 2.2.3): hardware modules such as HSMs and TPMs must be upgraded or redesigned for PQC because of larger keys and different computational needs, which may be done by updating firmware or hardware.
- [source] NCSC (A6): legacy long-lived infrastructure may not be capable of transition; industrial IoT may not be upgradeable; hardware acceleration for PQC was expected to arrive 2026-27.
- [source] RFC 9019 (A8) and CSWP 39 (A2): boot code in ROM cannot be changed, so its signature scheme must outlive the device. This is the main hardware-bound element for firmware signing.
- [source] Caliptra (A19, open-source silicon root of trust, datacenter-oriented, used here as a documented example not a drone part): 1.x silicon has an ECDSA-only identity chain rooted in ROM and no PQC accelerator. Release 1.3 adds a software-only ML-DSA-87 identity chain "entirely in Runtime Firmware", with no hardware or ROM changes, to avoid deprecating the installed base. Documented limits: the PQ chain does not attest Caliptra's own ROM or first-stage firmware; some commands have significant tail latency without acceleration; the per-device PQ seed is software-held and must not be provisioned in debug state; the first-stage firmware lacks the code and storage budget to take the seed early. Caliptra 2.x pairs with a hardware ML-DSA/ML-KEM accelerator.
- [inference] Caliptra is a worked example of the general pattern: a firmware update can add PQ signing/verification capability and a parallel PQ identity, but cannot retroactively make the immutable ROM-rooted classical chain quantum-safe, and the new chain may not cover everything the old chain covered. Whether that matches any drone SoC depends on the SoC.
- [source] wolfBoot (A16): hybrid verification needs a keystore holding both key types and larger image headers (see 3.2 sizes); OTP keystore provisioning tools exist in the changelog. [inference] Flash overhead for headers and bootloader code is the practical limit on an in-field bootloader update; no source gave a bootloader-size budget for a drone autopilot.
- [source] Banegas et al. (A20): in their RIOT case study the flash-optimised SHA3-256 was 1692 bytes versus 1008 for SHA-256, and a faster SHA3 variant was 11548 bytes; the authors say a 10 kB flash overhead is a significant increase on a roughly 50 kB image. Typical low-power MCU budgets of about 64 kB RAM and 500 kB flash are cited as not uncommon. Pre-standard context (2021).
- [source] UAV review (A21): onboard compute, memory, size and weight limit what PQC a UAV can add; hardware implementations for UAVs are an identified gap.
- [inference] Feasible by firmware update (if flash and RAM headroom exist and the bootloader itself is updatable): session-key hybrid KEM in the application link layer; PQ signature verification in an updatable second-stage bootloader or application-level update verifier; additional manifest signatures. Likely needs hardware change or replacement: boot ROM or fused/OTP-hashed public keys with only classical algorithms and no updatable stage before it; secure elements whose algorithm set is fixed; units with no flash headroom for larger signatures and headers. These classifications are mine; the sources support the underlying constraints (ROM immutability, fixed-function chips, flash limits) but none classifies drone parts.
- Secure elements and vendor datasheets with PQC limits (for example NXP, Infineon, ST): not retrieved in this stream.

## 5. Topic 4: HNDL, authenticity vs confidentiality, Mosca

### 5.1 Definitions and distinctions (sources)

- [source] IR 8547 ipd (A4): harvest now, decrypt later means adversaries collect encrypted data now to decrypt once quantum technology matures; sensitive data often retains value for years, which is a main reason the transition is urgent. Its examples of long-sensitivity data are government secrets and medical records.
- [source] CISA/NSA/NIST (A3): data with a long "secrecy lifetime" may be targeted now; the fact sheet uses "catch now, break later" and "harvest now, decrypt later" and asks for estimates of how long datasets need protection.
- [source] NCSC (A6): record the data you hold including its expected lifetime and value to an adversary; prioritise services processing the most valuable or long-lived data.
- [source] EU roadmap (A7): a confidentiality use case is high risk if compromise after 10 years or more would still cause significant damage; such data should be protected by end 2030. Confidentiality needing protection at all is medium or high risk.
- [source] IR 8547 ipd (A4, section 3.1.2): authentication has no harvest-now analogue; it remains secure as long as the algorithms and keys are secure at the time authentication is performed.
- [source] NCSC (A6, ICS section): for wirelessly connected field OT sensors, confidentiality might not need strong protection but integrity is likely critical, since faulty readings or commands can cause failures.
- [source] GRI 2025 (A22): if a CRQC arrives before migration finishes, integrity, availability and operational control across critical systems are at stake, not only confidentiality.
- [source] Banegas et al. (A20): see 3.1 for the prospective-forgery statement.
- The phrase "trust now, forge later": the search returned only secondary/commercial pages (not retrieved or cited). No primary source I retrieved uses the phrase. The primary-source substance is the IR 8547 and Banegas statements above.

### 5.2 Mosca inequality

- [source] GRI 2025 (A22, main text): attributes the framing to Mosca 2013 with three quantities: shelf-life time (years the information must stay protected), migration time, and threat time (years until relevant actors can break the vulnerable systems, "Q-Day"). If shelf-life plus migration exceeds threat time, the organisation may be unable to protect assets for the required time. It links this to harvest-now-decrypt-later and adds that integrity and operational control are also at stake.
- [source] IR 8547 ipd (A4, section 3): states the same relation in X and Y terms: X = years data must stay secure, Y = years needed to transition, and migration must start before X + Y exceeds the expected time Z to a cryptographically relevant quantum computer. It notes past cryptographic migrations took over a decade.

### 5.3 HNDL classification table: drone/robot data (INFERENCE ONLY, not sourced fact)

Labelled [inference]. Lifetimes are qualitative placeholders for the fleet owner to replace with real values; no source gives drone-specific lifetimes. The only numeric threshold taken from a source is the EU roadmap's 10-year line for "high" confidentiality risk. "Forgery window" is the period during which a future CRQC could be used to forge something that devices or systems would still accept, given the classical keys remain trusted.

| Data / asset | Confidentiality lifetime (inference) | Authenticity lifetime (inference) | Reasoning (inference) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Telemetry (attitude, battery, position) | Usually short; long if tied to sensitive sites or people | Short (a command or reading is acted on in real time) | Value decays quickly. Position tracks of identifiable people or critical sites can have long privacy value. Authenticity matters at the time of use, so only the present key security matters (IR 8547 3.1.2 logic). |
| Video / imagery | Medium to long when it shows people, facilities or critical infrastructure | Short to medium (evidentiary use may need integrity later) | Imagery can be reused or re-identified years later; this is a classic HNDL candidate if sent over classically key-exchanged links. Authenticity for evidence is a separate, longer need (timestamps, provenance). |
| Mission plans / waypoints / geofences | Medium to long for sensitive operations (inspection of critical assets, security patrols) | Short per mission | Reveals what the operator monitors and when. Forged plans matter only while a key is trusted. |
| Map / point-cloud / site models | Long for facilities, utilities, defence-adjacent or high-value sites | Medium (a poisoned map could mislead future runs) | 3D models of sites keep value for years and may be hard to change. Integrity of stored maps is a long-lived asset. |
| Customer / site data (contracts, access info, schedules) | Long (often contractual or regulatory) | Medium to long | Often in the same class as the data customers already ask to protect for 10+ years. Meets the EU high-risk definition if confidentiality loss after 10 years is damaging. |
| Firmware images | Medium (reveals vulnerabilities and proprietary IP; low if public) | Very long: the verification key outlives the device | The images themselves are rarely secret from a capable adversary. The issue is the signature trust anchor, which is the core "trust now, forge later" exposure: once a CRQC exists, an unreplaced classical anchor allows forged images (IR 8547 3.1.1; CSWP 39 4.5; EU roadmap). |
| Command-and-control commands | Short | Short per command, but the signing/authentication key's trusted life can be long | Past commands are not secret in a harmful way; forging future commands needs the classical authentication key to still be accepted. Symmetric MAC schemes (as in MAVLink signing) are not broken by Shor directly (inference), so the migration burden is on key provisioning and any asymmetric pieces. |
| Cryptographic keys (session, long-term, provisioning secrets) | Session keys: as long as the data they protect; long-term keys: their whole lifetime | Long-term identity and signing keys: lifetime of the fleet | Compromising a long-term key (including via a later quantum break of how it was exchanged) exposes everything it ever protected or authenticated. |
| Logs (flight logs, audit logs) | Short to long depending on content (location, operator IDs) | Medium to long (forensic and regulatory value) | Logs may need tamper-evidence for years even if not secret. Signing/hash-chain choices made now will be checked later. |
| ID / attestation certificates (device identity, DICE-like chains) | Low (public by design) | Very long if rooted in ROM or fused keys | Same shape as firmware signing: a forged identity is accepted if the classical root is still trusted. Caliptra shows a PQ chain can be added in firmware yet leave the ROM-rooted classical chain unchanged (A19). |

## 6. Topic 5: Quantum timeline uncertainty (no timeline asserted)

- [source] GRI Quantum Threat Timeline Report 2025 (A22; page dated 2026-03-09; 26 experts surveyed): on the report page, the respondents' view is that a CRQC is "quite possible (28-49%)" within 10 years and likely (51-70%) within 15. The report body says 19 of 26 experts gave more than 5% likelihood within 10 years and 13 of 26 gave about 50% or more; averaged "optimistic" and "pessimistic" readings of the answer bins give about 49% and 28% for 10 years, and the optimistic reading about 15% for 5 years. The report says this signals an upward shift versus recent surveys. The survey question asks about a computer able to factor a 2048-bit number in under 24 hours. Respondents also say covert research and unexpected acceleration are concrete possibilities; one respondent caps their own estimates at 70% because obstacles may be found. Expert opinion, not a forecast; the answer bins are broad.
- [source] Gidney 2025 (A23, arXiv v1, 2025-05-21): estimates a 2048-bit RSA integer could be factored in under a week by a quantum computer with under one million noisy qubits, versus the 2019 estimate of 8 hours with 20 million noisy qubits. Assumptions: square grid with nearest-neighbour connections, 0.1% gate error, 1-microsecond surface-code cycle, 10-microsecond control reaction time. This is a resource estimate, not a statement about when such a machine will exist.
- [source] Babbush et al. (A24, Google Research, 2026, abstract only): estimates Shor's algorithm for the 256-bit ECDLP with fewer than about 1200 logical qubits and under 90 million Toffoli gates (or under about 1450 logical qubits and 70 million Toffoli gates), and says such circuits could run in minutes on superconducting architectures with fewer than half a million physical qubits at 1e-3 error and planar connectivity. It uses a zero-knowledge proof so details are not disclosed. Abstract only; assumptions beyond those stated were not read.
- [source] Luo et al. (A25, arXiv v2 2026-04-18, abstract only): reports a reduction in logical qubits for 256-bit prime-field ECDLP to 1333 versus 2124 in a previous low-width implementation (Häner et al.). Preprint; not independently checked.
- [source] NIST IR 8547 ipd (A4): NSM-10 sets 2035 as the US goal for mitigating as much quantum risk as feasible; ECDSA and RSA at 112-bit strength proposed as deprecated after 2030 and all disallowed after 2035 (draft, in flux).
- [source] RFC 9019 (A8): states a worst-case quantum key-extraction horizon of about 2030 as of 2021. [inference] This is a historic, un-updated figure from a 2021 RFC and should not be used as a current estimate.
- [inference] For the report: present these as ranges of expert opinion and engineering estimates with dates, and use the agency dates (NCSC 2028/2031/2035, EU 2026/2030/2035, NIST 2030/2035 draft) as planning deadlines rather than predictions.

## 8. Notes for synthesis

- Strongest sourced claims: (1) boot-code verification keys cannot be updated, so scheme choice there must outlive the device (CSWP 39, RFC 9019, IR 8547, SP 800-208); (2) authentication has no HNDL analogue but has a prospective forgery risk (IR 8547, Banegas); (3) EU and NCSC give concrete planning dates that already include a requirement for PQ-signed update paths on long-lived products (EU).
- In flux: NIST IR 8547 (draft), SP 1800-38 (preliminary drafts only), SUIT MTI draft, TLS ML-KEM draft, EU roadmap (page and survey updates in September 2026), MCUboot and wolfBoot (moving master branches).
